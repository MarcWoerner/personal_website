## The Unlikely Pilgrimage of Harold Fry - Thoughts on a Book

I am writing this a few days after returning home from a hitchhiking trip from the Alps to the Atlantic coast. I used my overtime at work to go on a little summer adventure, where The Unlikely Pilgrimage of Harold Fry became a kind of companion along the way. The book, together with the travelling, showed me why it is important to give your mind the space to wander. In this text, I will share my approach to achieving that. 

The Unlikely Pilgrimage of Harold Fry follows Harold Fry, a retired man who receives a letter from Queenie, a woman he has not spoken to in 20 years. She tells him that she is suffering from cancer in a hospital on the other side of England. After writing a reply, Harold intends to mail it, but instead of stopping at the nearby mailbox, he keeps walking. Believing that as long as he continues his journey on foot, Queenie will stay alive, he decides to deliver the letter to her himself.

On the way, he is forced to step out of the daily routines and habits he has lived by for years. By nature, this way of travelling reduces the amount of comfort and convenience, but step by step, he also realizes how joyful such a simple life can be. As a consequence, he starts stripping away everything he does not really need. And as the physical world around him becomes simpler and clearer, so does his mind. He realizes that there is mental space to move in now. This space confronts Harold with the central questions of his life—questions he had successfully avoided in the past.

After Harold leaves home, his wife Maureen is initially upset and confused by his sudden departure. However, as time passes, she too is confronted with this new, unfamiliar situation. The hole Harold left in her daily life also creates space for thoughts and questions she had previously been able to keep at a distance. This triggers a similar process of reflection on her side.

Without going into too much detail, and to give you, as the reader, the freedom to experience the book without being spoiled too much, I want to use the situation Harold and Maureen find themselves in as a starting point for a question that arose for me about halfway through the book.

I am from another generation than the two of them but have things changed? Are we more aware of our life situation nowdays, or are we just as vulnerable to speeding through life, caught up in constant distraction?

When was the last time I asked myself the big questions? Not too often. And it is uncomfortable. It is not pleasant to question things you identify with, such as your behaviour, the people you surround yourself with, or the decisions you have made. So I feel that we, as humans—if I may extrapolate from my own experience—have a natural aversion to that kind of confrontation.

Yet somehow, our minds always seem to find ways to bring these important topics back into our lives naturally. Maybe through the little questions that arise while waiting in line at the local grocery store, or the bigger ones that surface on hikes or during long winter nights.

Keeping this in mind, I think there is a very concerning development that has evolved over the past ten years. Attention has become a resource that companies actively compete for. The mechanism of distraction is part of an ongoing process of optimization, using endlessly engaging and increasingly addictive content and algorithms with the goal of maximizing the time we spend on these platforms.

Consequently, the amount of time we have in which our minds can simply wander has decreased drastically. In short, my main concern is that we are not bored anymore... 

When we are bored, our minds have to do something to fill the void. And often, the most important questions and ideas arise from exactly that state.

But we have numbed the mechanism that helps us ask big, uncomfortable questions. As a result, we don't ask the questions that help us reflect and thrive in life because we are “too busy” (watching reels). 

It’s like climbing a ladder without asking whether it’s leaning against the right wall, because we’re so absorbed in climbing that we never stop to look.

But can this really last? Can we keep moving through life without ever stopping to reflect?

There are two potential ways this can evolve.

The first could be illustrated by the Eisenhower Decision Matrix, where the quadrant of things that are important but not urgent comes under increasing pressure because it has been ignored for decades (Fig. 1). Eventually, it will explode. Society has given the aftermath a name: a midlife crisis.





The other outcome would be more like what happens with Harold and Maureen. Life opens up a little. Retirement, more time, fewer distractions—and finally, there is enough space for the thoughts and questions you have been keeping at a distance to come to the surface.

But even the most trusting dog, if it has been kept in a little kennel for long enough, probably won’t simply walk out peacefully.

So what should we do to avoid these outcomes?

Reduce the pressure regularly. And that pressure can be reduced by giving your mind the space it needs to bring important topics back to the surface. We also shouldn’t rely on that happening by chance. We need to find ways to deliberately ask ourselves the important questions every now and then.

So what does that look like in practice?

I want to share the three approaches that have stuck with me over the past few years and that I have found most helpful.

1. Start with finding your Questions

What makes your life worth it? 
What is important for you? 
What do you enjoy most? 
What has the greates influence on your wellbeing? 
(...)

Take a blank sheet of paper and write down the questions that naturally come to mind. Don’t try to come up with the “right” questions or answer them immediately. The important part is that you find the questions that genuinely matter to you.

The next step is to revisit these questions at certain times during the year. I do it once or twice a year. Answering them can be tough and intense, so take the time you need, but be honest with yourself (being honest here is the toughest part from my experience). It can also help to find someone who knows you well and with whom you can share the experience. 

I personally have been doing a yearly review with friends around New Year’s for the past three years. We ask ourselves these questions together and look back at how we have changed, and whether and how we have aligned our lives with the ideas and priorities that emerge from those questions. 

In addition to that, I regularly meet up with my best friend to check in on life. These conversations give us space to ask ourselves questions like these, but also simply to catch up on how we are doing and what is going on in our lives. It is something like a shorter-scale version of the yearly review above. 


2. Create long phases to let things just flow 

Another thing that helps me a lot is creating phases of free time. Like, real free time. Not active holidays where you have everything planned out from the beginning. Just go somewhere, grab your close friends, and do something you enjoy.

In my case, these phases are parkour and camping trips where we just flow through cities and nature without any real plan. I only really realized how much these trips give me when I didn’t go on one last year. I noticed how much energy, creativity, and mental stability I had been getting from them without even consciously realizing it.

Don’t plan. Just go out and see what happens.

3. Get your attention back - or be bored more

The platforms we use are, not gonna lie, both a blessing and a curse. They can help us get inspired and stay connected, but most of the time, I would argue that they kill our drive and motivation.

The easiest fix would probably be to delete your accounts altogether. But then you also lose the positive sides of social media. I think the truth lies somewhere in the middle: find a way to interact with social media in a more controlled way.

Make it less available. Use it only on your computer, or on the device you use least in your daily routine. The goal should be to reduce your usage drastically. Log out of your accounts after using them, so that every time you want to open them, you have to ask yourself: “Is it really worth it right now?”

You can also plan a week or even a month without social media every now and then.

At first, you will probably notice yourself reaching for your phone almost automatically. You will unlock it without even thinking about it, only to realize that there is nothing you actually need to do.

And that is when something interesting happens. The boredom comes back.

Enjoy it!

Maybe boredom is the doorway into the “important but not urgent” part of the matrix.
